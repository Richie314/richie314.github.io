In my fifth year of high school, I investigated **Malus's law**: how the intensity of polarized light changes as we rotate a polarizing filter.
I built an apparatus using LEGO, a laser diode, two polarizers, a photoresistor, and an Arduino Uno.
The experiment brought together the physics of light and the electronics needed to measure it.

The most interesting part came when the first results did not follow the curve I expected.
I had to look beyond the optical setup and question how I was converting the sensor readings into light intensity.
Here, I'll walk through the experiment documented in my final report, the original measurements, and the limits of the interpretation I used at the time.

## 1. What we expect from two polarizers

A linearly polarized light wave has an electric field that oscillates along a fixed direction perpendicular to the direction of propagation.<d-cite key="polarization"></d-cite>

An ideal linear polarizer transmits the electric-field component parallel to its transmission axis.
If the incident field has amplitude $$E_0$$ and makes an angle $$\theta$$ with that axis, the transmitted amplitude is

$$
E = E_0\cos\theta.
$$

Because intensity is proportional to the square of the field amplitude, we obtain **Malus's law**:<d-cite key="malus-law"></d-cite>

$$
I(\theta)=I_0\cos^2\theta.
$$

In this ideal model, $$I_0$$ is the intensity of the linearly polarized beam immediately before the analyzer.
The angle $$\theta$$ is measured between the transmission axes of the two filters.
For the ideal case, parallel axes transmit the maximum, crossed axes transmit no light, and a 45° angle gives half the maximum.
This is the projection argument described in [OpenStax's explanation of polarization](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/1-7-polarization).

| Relative angle | Ideal transmitted fraction |
| --- | --- |
| 0° | 100% |
| 30° | 75% |
| 45° | 50% |
| 60° | 25% |
| 90° | 0% |
| 180° | 100% |

I suggest keeping the distinction between field amplitude and intensity in mind: the projection gives a cosine, while the intensity follows its square.
The pattern repeats every 180°, since reversing a filter's axis gives the same transmission direction.

## 2. Building the apparatus

My optical path was straightforward:

**Laser → fixed polarizer → rotating analyzer → photoresistor.**

The first filter stayed fixed relative to the laser, while I rotated the second filter to vary their relative angle.
The laser pointed downward through the two filters toward the sensor.
I built the support from white LEGO bricks, with separate holders for the filters and an enclosed compartment for the photoresistor.
The whole assembly sat inside an opaque cardboard box, and I took the measurements in a darkened room to reduce ambient light.

### The filters I actually used

The final report identifies the filters as ordinary **photographic circular polarizers, or CPL filters**, rather than laboratory linear polarizers.
That distinction matters when comparing the real apparatus with the ideal model above.
A typical CPL combines a linear polarizing element and a quarter-wave retarder; the retarder can change linear polarization into circular or elliptical polarization, depending on the light and orientation.
[Edmund Optics' polarizer selection guide](https://www.edmundoptics.com/knowledge-center/application-notes/optics/polarizer-selection-guide/) explains this construction.

<figure class="malus-photo malus-photo--compact">
  <img src="{{ '/assets/img/malus-law/photographic-filters.webp' | relative_url }}" width="1600" height="900" loading="lazy" alt="The pair of photographic CPL filters used in the experiment, shown in their protective cases.">
  <figcaption>The photographic filters used for the measurements.</figcaption>
</figure>

The direction each CPL faces and its response at the laser's wavelength can affect the polarization reaching the next filter.
CPLs introduce a sort of rotation into the light that passes through them; fortunately, the direction of the rotation is dependent on the face chosen as the "front" of the filter. Because the two filters are identical, we can **cancel out the effect of the CPL** components by putting one of the upside-down.

### How the Arduino acquired a reading

The detector was an **LDR**, or light-dependent resistor: its resistance decreases as more light reaches it.<d-cite key="photoresistor"></d-cite>
The Arduino Uno sampled the divider voltage on an analog input and sent the results over USB serial.

> <span class="sr-only">Information: </span>
>
> The code powering the Arduino is avaible in [this GitHub repo](https://github.com/Richie314/Laser-and-LDR-control-arduino/ "Go to the repo")
{: .block-info }

| Setting in the published sketch | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Raw readings per group | 20 |
| Delay after each raw reading | 5 ms |
| Values discarded from each sorted group | Three lowest and three highest |
| Group results averaged for one reported voltage | 20 |
| Delay after the laser-on command | 10 ms |
| Pause between groups | 2 s |

Each group therefore contributes a **trimmed mean of 14 readings**.
With 20 groups, the configured procedure acquires 400 raw readings and combines the 20 trimmed means into one reported voltage.
The acquisition loop calls its laser-control functions before and after each group, with the intention of limiting sensor heating.
The retained data do not include a measurement of the laser's actual on/off timing.
These are the settings preserved in the published code; the workbook retains the final averages, rather than the raw samples needed to reconstruct the scatter within each group.

The sketch converts the analog reading to voltage using the nominal 5 V reference and a divisor of 1024.
That corresponds to a nominal step of about $$4.88 mV$$ before averaging.
Averaging can produce stored voltages with more decimal places, but those decimal places do not establish an equally small absolute measurement uncertainty.

## 3. From Arduino voltage to resistance

The circuit used a nominal $$5 V$$ supply and a $$100 kΩ$$ fixed resistor in series with the LDR.
The fixed-resistor value appears in both the presentation's circuit diagram and the spreadsheet formulas.
The voltage was measured across the LDR, rather than across the fixed resistor.<d-cite key="voltage-divider"></d-cite>
The circuit diagram and the photograph below show the voltage-divider arrangement and its physical implementation inside the LEGO detector compartment.

  <figcaption>The LDR and voltage-divider circuit inside the detector compartment.</figcaption>

Calling the measured voltage $$V$$, the supply $$V_s$$, and the fixed resistance $$R_f$$, the divider gives

$$
V=V_s\frac{R_{\mathrm{LDR}}}{R_f+R_{\mathrm{LDR}}}.
$$

Solving for the sensor resistance gives

$$
R_{\mathrm{LDR}}=R_f\frac{V}{V_s-V}.
$$

For example, the first set's reading at 0° was $$0.4494 V$$.
Using the nominal circuit values, we obtain

$$
R_{\mathrm{LDR}}=100\,\mathrm{k\Omega}\times\frac{0.4494}{5-0.4494}
\approx9.8756\,\mathrm{k\Omega}.
$$

The direction of the response can seem surprising at first: more light lowers the LDR resistance, which also lowers the voltage measured across it.
We therefore cannot treat the voltage itself as a quantity proportional to light intensity.

## 4. Checking the laser before testing the law

Before interpreting the two-filter measurements, I rotated a single filter relative to the stationary laser and support.
The detected signal changed with orientation.

This chart uses *normalized inverse resistance as a sensor proxy*, rather than a calibrated intensity measurement.

Keeping the first filter fixed kept its orientation relative to the laser unchanged while I rotated only the analyzer.
The familiar statement that a polarizer transmits half the incident intensity applies to ideal, unpolarized incident light; it should not be assumed for this laser.
For the angular comparison, I instead worked with relative signals.

## 5. The first conversion did not reproduce the expected curve

My initial analysis assumed a simple inverse relationship between resistance and light level $$L$$:

$$
R=\frac{k}{L}.
$$

If that model were adequate, an unknown constant $$k$$ would cancel when taking a ratio:

$$
\frac{L}{L_{\mathrm{ref}}}=\frac{R_{\mathrm{ref}}}{R}.
$$

The report initially used a reference measurement with only the fixed polarizer in place.
For Set 1, that reference was $$0.4492 V$$, corresponding to approximately $$9.8708 kΩ$$.
The workbook also normalized each two-filter sweep to its own inferred maximum, using the minimum resistance in that sweep.
I use this second convention consistently in the comparison charts:

$$
y_{\mathrm{inverse}}=\frac{R_{\min}}{R}.
$$

This normalization makes the maximum equal to one, but it does not establish an absolute light calibration or measure the analyzer's insertion loss.
The first sweep shows why that distinction matters.

| Angle | Measured voltage (V) | Derived resistance (kΩ) | Inverse-model ratio | Ideal $$\cos^2\theta$$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| −45° | 0.4950 | 10.9878 | 0.8988 | 0.5000 |
| 0° | 0.4494 | 9.8756 | 1.0000 | 1.0000 |
| 30° | 0.5097 | 11.3511 | 0.8700 | 0.7500 |
| 45° | 0.5535 | 12.4480 | 0.7934 | 0.5000 |
| 60° | 0.6676 | 15.4095 | 0.6409 | 0.2500 |
| 90° | 4.0088 | 404.4391 | 0.0244 | 0.0000 |

At 45°, this conversion suggests roughly 79% of the maximum, whereas the ideal prediction is 50%.
The differences are too large to describe as a close quantitative match.
The readings at −45° and +45° also differ, even though the ideal curve is symmetric.
A sensor conversion alone cannot explain every feature of the data.

## 6. Trying a different sensor model

I then explored an empirical exponential conversion:

$$
L=\alpha e^{-\beta R}.
$$

Normalizing to the same sweep's maximum removes $$\alpha$$:

$$
y_{\mathrm{exponential}}=\frac{L}{L_{\max}}
=e^{\beta(R_{\min}-R)}.
$$

When resistance is expressed in ohms, the equivalent value is $$4.7\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{\Omega}^{-1}$$.
The units must match so that the exponent is dimensionless.

{% include malus-plot.liquid plot='set_1' caption='Set 1: the same voltage readings converted using two sensor models, with ±2° horizontal error bars.' %}

The red points use the original inverse model, while the green points use the exponential model.
The blue line is the ideal prediction, and the horizontal bars show the report's ±2° angular uncertainty.
Both sets of points come from *the same measured voltages*.
The curve changes because the conversion changes, rather than because I collected a new set of readings.

The exponential model brings many points closer to the expected shape, but substantial discrepancies remain.
For example, the converted values at −45° and +45° are approximately 0.593 and 0.298, compared with a theoretical value of 0.5 for both.
I would describe this as improved agreement under an empirical model, rather than a precise verification of the law.

An LDR is not automatically a calibrated light meter.
Its response depends on the particular sensor and the illumination conditions, as discussed in [Adafruit's photocell guide](https://learn.adafruit.com/photocells?view=all).
It is important to clarify that the exponential expression here is an exploratory approximation from my original project, **not a universal law for photoresistors**.

## 7. Repeated measurements and Set 4

The final report includes two further sweeps analyzed with the same value of $$\beta$$.
They cover 0° to 180°, rather than the −90° to +90° interval used in Set 1.

{% include malus-plot.liquid plot='set_2' plot2='set_3' caption='Sets 2 and 3: both sensor conversions compared with the ideal curve, retaining all readings.' %}

The final report covers the laser control and Sets 1-3.
A later measurement workbook adds **Set 4**, recorded under different conditions.
I include that follow-up here as supplementary data, rather than as a measurement set presented in the final report.
It uses **$$\beta=0.07\,\mathrm{k\Omega}^{-1}$$**, rather than 0.47.
The spreadsheet notes better shielding from ambient light and warmer, more humid conditions as possible reasons for differences between runs; these were proposed explanations, rather than separately measured causes.
It also flags possible positioning errors in the first and last readings.

{% include malus-plot.liquid plot='set_4' caption='Set 4: both sensor models and all thirteen readings, including the flagged endpoints.' %}

For this chart, I shifted Set 4's recorded angles from 90°-270° to −90°-90° by subtracting 180°.
This leaves the theoretical cosine-squared values unchanged.
I kept all 13 measurements, including the two flagged endpoints.

To compare the curves numerically for this article, I recalculated the **root-mean-square deviation** from the ideal prediction:

$$
\mathrm{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
\left(y_i-\cos^2\theta_i\right)^2}.
$$

Each row below uses all 13 points, with equal weight and the workbook's original $$\beta$$ value.
These are descriptive deviations in normalized units, not uncertainty estimates or formal goodness-of-fit tests.

| Measurement set | Inverse-model RMSE | Exponential-model RMSE | $$\beta$$ (kΩ⁻¹) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Set 1 | 0.278 | 0.116 | 0.47 |
| Set 2 | 0.266 | 0.058 | 0.47 |
| Set 3 | 0.205 | 0.070 | 0.47 |
| Set 4 | 0.058 | 0.056 | 0.07 |

The exponential conversion reduces the deviation in the first three sets, while Set 4 shows only a small difference between the two conversions.
The change in $$\beta$$ is also a reason to avoid treating it as a transferable sensor calibration.

## 8. What the experiment could establish

My final report concluded that the experiment verified Malus's law after using the empirical light-to-resistance conversion.
Its tables and plots document the same Sets 1-3 shown here; they do not supply a separate, independently calibrated intensity measurement.
Looking back at the retained measurements, I would distinguish that original conclusion from the more limited claim the data can establish.

The experiment showed a repeatable dependence of the detected signal on the relative orientation of the filters.
Its broad pattern is consistent with the expected transmission maximum for aligned filters and minimum for crossed filters.
It also exposed how strongly an inferred intensity curve can depend on the detector model.

There is an important limit to the calibration argument: I selected an empirical conversion because it made the data resemble the law I wanted to test.
That agreement cannot independently validate both Malus's law and the sensor conversion.
An independent calibration would be needed to separate those questions.

Although the firmware documents how readings were averaged, the archived tables do not retain the individual readings or group results needed to estimate their scatter.
They also do not provide a complete voltage uncertainty budget or an independently measured light-to-resistance calibration.
For that reason, I have not added vertical error bars or claimed agreement within experimental uncertainty.
The recorded decimal places describe the stored readings, rather than establishing their accuracy.

The divider also becomes sensitive to voltage error as its reading approaches the supply:

$$
\frac{dR}{dV}=R_f\frac{V_s}{(V_s-V)^2}.
$$

That matters near crossed polarizers, where a high LDR resistance pushes the measured voltage toward 5 V.
